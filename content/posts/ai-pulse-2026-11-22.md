Two days ago the Riemann Hypothesis claim was a 17.4-million-line tarball nobody could read. Today it has a certificate. That inversion — verification infrastructure outracing human comprehension — runs through everything below, and it's the cleanest marker yet of where this decade is going.

## The referees finished before the readers started

The Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative issued a joint statement Friday certifying Levent Alpöge's Claude-assisted proof of the Riemann Hypothesis: "full confidence it is correct." MIT's Andrew Sutherland ran all 175,187 modules through the Lean kernel plus three independent checkers — total elapsed time under 35 minutes across tools — with zero errors, and Waterloo's Rubinstein reproduced the checks independently. The theorem statement was confirmed identical to Mathlib's `RiemannHypothesis` by two separate comparator tools, and no circular assumptions were found. Elliot Glazer adds that the formalization appears to cover the *generalized* hypothesis, with under a 1% chance of a Lean bug.

Here's the part no other outlet is saying plainly: the certification took ~48 hours, and the human-readability phase hasn't even begun. No living mathematician has organized those 17 million lines into prose. "Correct but unreadable by any single person" is now a real category of mathematical knowledge, and the community just legitimized it with an official instrument — multi-checker compilation plus independent human replication. Meanwhile Alessandro Masullo's competing 940-page human proof, twelve years in the making, arrived the same week as a PDF awaiting referees. The AI-assisted claimant skipped that queue entirely. The race dynamics have inverted: machine-checkable form now beats human review at its own game, and 167 years of failed attempts have two live candidates with no agreed process for adjudicating between them. That process — not the Clay money — is the actual historical event.

## Models that edit their own minds

A paper titled "Language Models Can Rewrite Their Own Weights" (arXiv, v2 viral on HN's front page) demonstrates the first model modifying its own parameters through its own reasoning — no gradients, no retraining run, the frozen-weights paradigm quietly broken. An open 27B model gained +6.6 points on unseen problems by rewriting itself.

Everyone will read this as "self-improving AI," which is true but not the sharp edge. The sharp edge is deployment economics: if deployed models can improve in place, the entire retrain-redistribute industrial complex — the thing eating a trillion dollars of capex right now — has a competitor it didn't expect. And the safety question flips from "what did you train?" to "what has it done to itself since you shipped it?" — a question no current auditing regime is built to answer, because audits assume weights are static artifacts. They just stopped being that.

## AI is now writing the chips that train AI

OpenAI reported that its models rewrote AMD flagship GPU circuit code — SystemVerilog RTL rewrites, verified behavior-preserving by Synopsys tooling, up to 14% PPA improvement over heavily-optimized human baselines, with some changes already in silicon. Roughly a year of expected design progress landed in under five months.

Treat the numbers as claims-tier — every figure is OpenAI's, AMD has published none, and "in silicon" is unaudited. But the structural point survives the skepticism: this is the highest-leverage confirmation yet of a literal feedback loop, where AI improves the physical compute frontier that trains the next AI. Anthropic's roadshow claim of porting Claude to AMD GPUs in three days with ~100% AI-written kernels was the software version of this; now the hardware side is on the table too. The insight nobody is drawing: if model vendors start treating chip design as an in-house AI workload, the separation between "AI company" and "chip company" — the separation NVIDIA's entire market position rests on — gets a countdown clock.

## Drug screening at 25 milliseconds per molecule

Genesis Therapeutics released RUBY, a potency-prediction model built on their Pearl cofolding foundation model, and it beats every open and closed competitor across OpenFE, CASP16, FEP+ 4 and OpenBind — at 25 milliseconds per ligand, about 1,250x faster than Boltz-2 at equal enrichment, with audited training data released CC BY-SA 4.0.

The quietly radical move is the data license. Every other frontier lab in this space treats training data as the moat; Genesis published theirs under a share-alike license the same day Reuters reported Novo Nordisk cutting design-make-test cycles from eight to three. When the accuracy is commoditized and the data is public, the pricing power in computational chemistry collapses — the same squeeze the open-weights crowd is putting on foundation-model APIs, arriving in drug discovery with a two-year lag. The hit-ID/lead-optimization split that structured the entire industry's org charts no longer needs two toolchains.

## Sixteen living viruses, designed end to end

In Science this week: Arc Institute and Stanford chemically synthesized 285 phage genomes generated by the Evo model, and 16 of them turned out to be fully functional, self-replicating viruses — the first AI-designed complete viral genomes demonstrated to work as autonomous organisms. Combinations of the AI phages overcame bacterial resistance that defeated a natural phage.

A 5.6% viability rate sounds low until you remember the baseline for "type out an entire working virus genome from scratch" was previously zero. The therapeutic read is genuinely hopeful: phage cocktails that outpace resistance evolution could be generated on demand. But the same paper is the strongest biosecurity forcing function yet published — and the honest caveat is that novelty versus the training set was never measured, so we don't know if Evo designed these or remembered them. That's exactly the verification gap, showing up in a domain where the wet lab is the only referee.
