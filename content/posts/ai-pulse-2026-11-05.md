The most important number published this week is $1,279. That's roughly what it cost OpenAI's autonomous cyber pipeline, per vulnerability, to find a bug in the Linux kernel, write a working exploit for it, and write the fix — nine hundred and forty-eight times in a row. The rest of today's digest is about what happens when that kind of economics stops being surprising: you start needing cryptography to know who you're talking to, and benchmarks that can't be marketed.

## The kernel has a fuzzing problem now

OpenAI's ["A thousand fewer paths to root"](https://openai.com/index/a-thousand-fewer-paths-to-root/) is the story of the day, arguably of the month. A prototype pipeline built around an upcoming cyber-focused model scanned a stock hardened Linux kernel (~2.8M lines), generated 2,308 candidate findings, dynamically reproduced 1,724 of them, and shipped 948 verified local privilege-escalation exploits **with patches**, for a total run cost of $1.2M — about $1,279 per vulnerability, against historical bounty valuations of $18.9M–$66.3M for comparable LPE classes. It also surfaced 10+ novel exploitation techniques. Every result went through human review and coordinated disclosure via the "Patch the Planet" partners, which is the correct way to do this — but the interesting part isn't the disclosure process, it's the shape of the funnel: candidates in, validated exploits out, humans as reviewers of a batch. Kernel vuln research has historically been gated on a small pool of people who could hold both the source tree and exploitation primitives in their head at once; that gate is now open to anyone who can rent the compute.

**The read**: the threat model inverts. "Low-privileged account on an unpatched box ≈ root" is now true for any resourced actor, and the only structural defense is defense that's as automated as the offense. OpenAI's own framing — "paths to root" — is apt: this doesn't mean the kernel is suddenly safe, it means the gap between the haves and have-nots of this capability is now measured in months, not decades. ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/a-thousand-fewer-paths-to-root/))

## Anthropic's answer to a question nobody asked yet

With agent-to-agent and agent-to-human traffic exploding, a new attack class is coming: **model impersonation**. Anthropic's [FlashProve system](https://www.anthropic.com/research/provable-inference) — built with EPFL/StarkWare/MIT collaborators — wraps model inference in a zkSNARK, so a response carries a cryptographic proof that it came from the model that claims to have generated it. This isn't a deepfake-detection heuristic or a watermark; it's a mathematical statement. As routing and proxying become the norm (and the CPS precedent from Monday's digest shows how easily "the AI said" gets laundered through a chain of custody), provable inference is the infrastructure that keeps model identity from dissolving into the network stack. Their demo page is characteristically direct: "when you talk to an AI model you have almost no ways to know it's really them."

**The read**: this is the second half of a deliberate sequence — Anthropic's Nov-31 model-identity work was detection, this is proof. What's notable is that they're building it before the attack exists at scale, which is how you build trust infrastructure: not as a patch, but as a floor. ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/provable-inference))

## StateBench: transformers can't track state, and marketing can't fix that

Ai2's [StateBench](https://allenai.org/blog/statebench) is the quiet bombshell of the week. Four frontier models — Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Sol/Luna — fall to ≤13% accuracy on long-document state tracking at the hardest ("general finite") level, regardless of how much long-context marketing surrounds them. Meanwhile, recurrent and hybrid architectures with Gated DeltaNet generalize up to 250x beyond their training length on the same task. Transformers, notably, fail even when trained directly on it.

**The read**: this is the cleanest architecture-level result we've seen — not a leaderboard reshuffle but a structural ceiling. When a 13% success rate coexists with million-token input windows in the same product generation, "long context" isn't a capability, it's an input limit. ([Ai2](https://allenai.org/blog/statebench))

## Ripple-65B: capability getting cheaper faster than it's getting better

Ricursive — the AlphaChip team's new company — shipped [Ripple-65B](https://www.ricursive.com/ripple), a scratch-trained open-weight coding model that hits 80.52 on Terminal-Bench 2.1, claiming US open-weight SOTA for agentic coding at ~$32M total training cost, roughly a tenth of what comparable open coding models have cost. Self-reported benchmark, usual caveats apply, but the trend isn't self-reported: the price of frontier-adjacent capability keeps falling faster than the frontier rises. ([Ricursive](https://www.ricursive.com/ripple))

**The read**: the ripple effect (pun unavoidable) hits the API-margin business first. If a $32M scratch build can compete on agentic coding, the moat around frontier labs shifts further from "who has the biggest model" toward "who has the trust infrastructure" — which is exactly what FlashProve is building. Nice convergence with today's second story.

## AlphaFold3's second act: reading the immune system

TGen and City of Hope repurposed AlphaFold3 to predict **T-cell receptor–antigen recognition** from sequence alone — the MHC:peptide:TCR triple interaction — with enough accuracy to flag which immune cells see a given pathogen or cancer target ([Science Advances](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261103/AlphaFold3-reveals-molecular-interactions-driving-T-cell-recognition.aspx)). This is TCR-screening work that currently takes weeks of bench time compressed into hours of compute, and it's a proof that frontier models aren't single-purpose tools but general-purpose molecular reasoning engines that get repurposed.

**The read**: nobody at DeepMind designed AlphaFold3 for T-cell recognition — the community bent it to a new question. That's the real story of AI-accelerated science in 2026: not one model discovering one thing, but a thousand scientists discovering a thousand uses for the same model. ([news-medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20261103/AlphaFold3-reveals-molecular-interactions-driving-T-cell-recognition.aspx))
