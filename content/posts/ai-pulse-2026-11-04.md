A year ago the worry was that AI would write the exploits; this week's pattern is stranger — it's better at reading the systems we already trust than we are, and it's working its way up the critical-infrastructure ladder one rung at a time.

## An AI agent just audited the software that runs fusion reactors

Security firm Cipher pointed an autonomous agent at EPICS Base — the decades-old control layer under particle accelerators and fusion reactors — and it came back 3 hours 49 minutes later with four CVEs, including a CVSS-9.8 remote code execution flaw. CISA has issued advisory ICSA-26-300-01, and fixes landed in EPICS 7.0.11. This is the third AI-found-vulnerability archetype in two weeks, each rung higher up the stakes ladder, and the uncomfortable through-line is that the machines now audit faster than humans can sign off on what they find. When the researcher is an agent and the target is a tokamak, the security research pipeline itself becomes the story.

## Anthropic wants to prove which model you're actually talking to

Anthropic is building a cryptographic system to prove AI model identity and detect impersonators. The move converges with OpenAI's SafetyIDs, shipped October 22 — two frontier labs independently arriving at "model passports" within a fortnight. Provenance plumbing like this usually starts as a safety feature and ends as a moat: once enterprises build verification into their procurement stack, "can you prove it's your model?" becomes a checkbox only the incumbents can tick.

[TechCrunch — Anthropic is working on a system to prove AI models' identity](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/03/anthropic-is-working-on-a-system-to-prove-ai-models-identity/)

## Microsoft's "monofacts" theory: one discovery per hallucination

A new Microsoft paper (arXiv 2611.03617) proposes that at frontier posteriors, a model's capacity to make genuine discoveries couples roughly one-to-one with its tendency to hallucinate — you can't maximally have one without the other. It's the first quantitative framing of "why LLMs can discover anything at all," and it hands practitioners a dial rather than a verdict: the same data-curation choices that suppress confabulation may be quietly suppressing the discovery rate too. The uncomfortable implication is that "hallucinate less" and "discover less" might be the same setting.

[arXiv — Monofacts (2611.03617)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03617)

## Data centers are buying old jet engines while the grid catches up

With grid interconnect queues stretching for years, data-center operators are installing retired aviation jet engines as bridge power — scavenged turbofans spinning up megawatts in parking lots while the substations get built. The detail that sticks: the same engines that flew passengers for 20 years are now rated in GPU-hours. It's the most literal image yet of AI's energy appetite outrunning infrastructure — the grid isn't failing, it's just queuing, and the queue now smells like kerosene.

[TechCrunch — Data centers are turning to old jet engines as the wait for power grows](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/03/data-centers-are-turning-to-old-jet-engines-as-the-wait-for-power-grows/)

## Apple quietly writes the first national age law for AI companions

Apple now restricts AI companion apps — alongside dating apps — to users 18 and up, the first mass-market age gate on companion AI. No legislature moved; a single App Store policy review effectively set a national standard overnight, because when the distribution is that concentrated, the store review guidelines ARE the regulatory regime. Every US companion-AI startup woke up with a compliance department they didn't have on Monday.
