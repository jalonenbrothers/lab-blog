# The Power Behind the Prompt: Can the AI Boom Grow Without Overloading the World Around It?

*The global electricity figures look manageable. The local consequences are another matter.*

A server rack is not much to look at. Behind the door of a data centre, it stands among rows of near-identical cabinets, surrounded by cooling equipment and thick electrical infrastructure. In the AI economy, however, this mundane-looking object is becoming a political question. Who supplies its electricity? Who funds the grid connection? What happens to the water supply, the cost of power, the carbon budget and the town nearby?

The most revealing fact about the AI energy boom is that two apparently contradictory propositions can both be true. **Data centres remain a relatively modest share of global electricity consumption. In some places they are becoming an enormous share of *new* demand.** That distinction is easy to miss, and central to getting the story right.

In April 2026, the International Energy Agency (IEA) updated its global outlook. It estimated that data centres used about **485 terawatt-hours (TWh) in 2025** and projected **roughly 950 TWh by 2030**—around 3% of global electricity demand. AI-focused centres would grow considerably faster than the overall category, but the estimate covers data centres generally, not just generative AI. The agency also warned that bottlenecks in power infrastructure, chip manufacturing and finance could hold back even the projects currently planned. [1](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary)

Now compare a more local figure. A June 2026 report by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory projected **649 TWh of US data-centre electricity consumption in 2030** in its reference case, with a wide compounded-uncertainty range of **521–843 TWh**. That corresponded to **9.5–15.3% of US electricity consumption** across the report's scenarios, with 11.8% in the reference case. [2](https://bies.lbl.gov/publications/united-states-data-center-energy-2025)

These forecasts are not interchangeable. They use different models and geographic scopes; neither is a meter reading of 2030. Indeed, their tension is instructive: there are substantial uncertainties in how much AI computing will be built, how heavily it will be used, and where it will be located. One should not blithely add the US forecast to the IEA global number, or present either as destiny. What they agree on is the direction of travel and the scale of the planning problem.

## The wrong question is whether the world has enough electricity

At global scale, a rise to 3% of electricity demand sounds absorbable. It might be. But electricity is not delivered by a single planetary socket. A data centre requires a particular quantity of reliable power at a particular site, connected through substations, transformers and transmission lines that have their own constraints and approval processes.

A region can generate substantial renewable electricity over a year and still lack power at the hours or locations a new customer needs it. The opposite can also occur: a grid may have enough energy in aggregate but not enough equipment to connect one concentrated new load. The crucial variables include **peak megawatts, hourly demand, grid congestion and the time needed to build infrastructure**—not simply annual TWh.

The IEA's October 2026 study, *Powering Artificial Intelligence*, reviewed policy and regulatory experience across 34 jurisdictions representing roughly 90% of global data-centre capacity. Its framing is telling: the challenge is not merely producing more energy, but integrating data centres without sacrificing affordability, energy security or sustainability. [3](https://www.iea.org/reports/powering-artificial-intelligence)

For a local household, a promised AI investment might mean construction jobs, a larger tax base and new commercial demand. For the same household it might also raise fears that scarce grid capacity, water or public infrastructure is being diverted to a highly profitable operator. Those concerns cannot be settled by pointing to a national average.

A fair test is straightforward to state, though harder to apply: **would the project still look beneficial if all the grid upgrades, water impacts, backup power, emissions and other local costs were fully counted—and if the community could see who pays for each?**

## China, America and Europe do not face the same energy puzzle

The phrase “the race for AI power” invites a crude competition in generating capacity. But installed capacity is not actual generation. A gigawatt of solar and a gigawatt of gas generation have different hourly profiles; neither says much about the local transmission network. A region with more annual electricity production may still have a worse bottleneck near its largest city.

The US is where the data-centre demand shock is especially concentrated. Large clusters of facilities must compete for grid connections and electrical equipment. Developers may respond by seeking dedicated generation, including gas-fired plants. But building power beside the servers is not a magical shortcut: it also requires turbines, fuel supply, emissions permits, backup capacity and reliability engineering. In the IEA's updated 2026 assessment, reliable on-site gas generation may require substantially more installed capacity than the connected data-centre load. [1](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary)

China brings a different combination: vast manufacturing capability, rapid power construction, regional imbalance and a historically coal-heavy electricity system. The IEA's 2025 analysis estimated that coal supplied nearly 70% of the electricity physically serving Chinese data centres at the time, while renewables and nuclear were projected to gain ground. This was an estimate of *physical electricity supply*, not the renewable energy that operators may have purchased contractually. [4](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-supply-for-ai)

Europe's challenge is shaped by another set of trade-offs: decarbonisation commitments, variable electricity prices, grid connection queues, land constraints and the need for reliable supply. The IEA projected renewables and nuclear to provide most of the additional electricity consumed by European data centres through 2030. [4](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-supply-for-ai) But neither that regional projection nor a corporate renewable power-purchase agreement can establish that a particular European site operates with low emissions every hour.

The strategic lesson is not that one region is winning and another is doomed. It is that **energy security, manufacturing strength, planning systems and geography are now part of AI competitiveness**.

## Can wind and solar supply the demand?

The answer depends on what “supply” means.

If the question is whether adding wind and solar can generate a very large quantity of electricity over a year, the answer is yes. These are mature, deployable technologies. If the question is whether a specific data centre can be powered exclusively by nearby wind and solar at every moment, without storage, transmission, demand flexibility or other sources, the answer is usually no. That is not an indictment of renewables; it is a statement about the difference between energy and continuous reliability.

It also explains why “100% renewable” claims need interrogating. A company can buy renewable electricity certificates or sign long-term contracts covering an annual volume of consumption, while the data centre still draws from a grid whose generation mix varies by hour. Such purchases can support new clean generation, especially if additionality is demonstrated. They are not necessarily equivalent to physically carbon-free supply in every operating hour.

There are several credible strategies, none sufficient on its own:

**Build clean generation.** Wind and solar reduce dependence on fossil fuels and can be built relatively quickly in suitable locations. Their value depends on access to the grid, timing and how much they displace carbon-intensive electricity.

**Build transmission and flexibility.** More interconnection, grid reinforcement, demand response and storage allow systems to use more low-carbon power and manage peaks. These upgrades are often less glamorous than a new power plant, but can be decisive.

**Use firm low-carbon power where available and economically justified.** Existing nuclear, hydro and geothermal can provide dependable supply; new nuclear may contribute in some systems, though finance and build times remain material constraints. Fusion is a legitimate research prospect, not a dependable source for the current decade's construction schedules.

**Make computing more flexible.** Not every computation is equally urgent. Some training, batch processing and data tasks could move in time or place to exploit cleaner, cheaper electricity. That will require genuinely flexible software and honest accounting of service constraints; interactive uses and certain workloads cannot simply be postponed.

**Improve efficiency—and account for rebound.** More efficient chips, models and cooling reduce energy per task, but cheaper computation can also unlock more usage. The IEA noted steep recent improvements in simple-query efficiency alongside the rise of more energy-intensive reasoning, video and agentic applications. Energy efficiency is necessary; it does not guarantee total demand will fall. [1](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary)

## The water question cannot be answered with a number per prompt

Viral claims about how many bottles of water a chatbot uses per question are often presented with more certainty than the underlying evidence warrants. Water demand depends on the data centre's cooling design, the local weather, the electricity generation mix and the task performed. It also matters whether a figure means water **withdrawn**, water **consumed** (for example, through evaporation), direct facility use or indirect use associated with generating electricity.

A 2025 peer-reviewed analysis by researchers including Lawrence Berkeley scientists found that workload-level water use can vary by more than **10,000-fold** across conditions. This is precisely why a universal water-per-prompt figure is misleading. [5](https://eta.lbl.gov/publications/water-use-data-center-workloads)

But variability does not make the problem imaginary. Another study in *Nature Sustainability* modelled potentially substantial US water and emissions footprints for AI servers under differing expansion and decarbonisation scenarios. The numbers are scenario estimates, not observed totals. The study also found mitigation possibilities from better siting and operational choices, while emphasising infrastructure limits. [6](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41893-025-01681-y)

The ethical question is particularly sharp in water-stressed regions: should a data centre that creates relatively few permanent jobs receive priority access to scarce water over residents, farms or ecosystems? There is no single global answer; there should be transparent local data and a public process capable of saying no.

## AI might also help solve the problems it creates

An honest account has to consider the other side of the ledger. AI can help forecast electricity demand, detect equipment faults, optimise transmission and manage flexible loads. Energy UK highlighted practical applications in network planning, maintenance and smarter charging in a report released on 2 November 2026. The report comes from an industry association and its case studies should be treated accordingly: they show possible uses, not independently established system-wide savings. [7](https://www.energy-uk.org.uk/publications/smarter-thinking-harnessing-ai-for-a-cleaner-fairer-energy-system/)

AI could also accelerate battery research, materials discovery and engineering design. Yet an AI-enabled discovery has no climate value until it survives laboratory replication, manufacturing, permitting, installation and operation. The same discipline must be applied to the positive claims as to the negative ones.

Here is a useful counterfactual test: if AI reduces a power system's emissions or costs, how much of that benefit is *additional* to what competent conventional software and investments could already achieve? If it enables more fossil-fuel extraction, greater consumption or slower retirement of carbon-intensive plants, the net effect could point the other way. “AI for climate” is not automatically the same thing as climate benefit.

## A better bargain for host communities

AI's electricity footprint is neither an apocalyptic impossibility nor an insignificant rounding error. The global perspective invites complacency; the local perspective can invite panic. Neither is sufficient on its own.

A more defensible policy would require developers and authorities to disclose, before approval, the facility's expected annual energy use, peak power demand, hourly carbon implications where estimable, water demand by season, backup-generation plans, connection upgrades and expected public costs. Actual figures should then be reported after opening, allowing promises to be checked. Forecasts should show low, central and high cases rather than a single attractive number.

Connection rules could reward genuinely flexible computing loads and developers willing to fund attributable upgrades, while protecting other customers from unjustified cost shifts. Clean-energy purchases should disclose whether they add capacity and how closely generation matches consumption. Host communities should have a meaningful say in siting and mitigation—not merely a press release announcing investment.

This is also a test of what we believe AI progress is for. If a technology promises productivity and scientific discovery, it should be possible to explain why a particular installation creates more public value than the resources it consumes. The answer may often be yes. But the burden of showing it should not fall solely on the residents next to the substation.

**The most important question is not whether we can power AI. It is whether we can do so without quietly transferring the bill, the risk and the environmental cost to everyone else.**
