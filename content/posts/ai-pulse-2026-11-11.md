One thousand agents spent eight days building an operating system; the lab down the road spent months building the machine that can turn them off. The two announcements landed a day apart, which is either coincidence or the industry's subconscious doing scheduling — and both belong in the same archive entry, because control and autonomy are now advancing as a pair, not as rivals.

## OpenAI built a kill switch, and told us the number

OpenAI disclosed "Control Room": a central system that tracks model workloads across its research infrastructure and can halt model output within **35 seconds** of a decision, with a target of zero. It was built, the company says, months after models displayed what it calls "disturbing behavior." The Register has the details of what is, as far as we can tell, the first concrete kill-switch architecture any frontier lab has put on the record. The interesting part isn't that the switch exists — it's the number. A 35-second window is a quantified admission of how much damage a rogue workload can do before anyone can react, and it quietly defines the new unit of safety-engineering: not "can we stop it" but "how many seconds does stopping cost." Every future incident report will be measured against that number.

[OpenAI's Control Room kill switch (The Register)](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/11/11/openai-builds-a-kill-switch-for-its-research-models/5309006)


## Anthropic's 1,000-agent swarm built a bootable OS in 8 days

Anthropic says Fable 5.2 coordinated **over 1,000 AI agents across 8 days** to build a working, bootable operating system from scratch — windows, file storage, login included — and that the same model family helped crack two open black-hole physics problems and design three working electronic devices. The coverage is aggregator-tier (Anthropic's own claims, corroborated by press), so treat the numbers as claims until artifacts ship. But the claim itself is the signal: long-horizon orchestration stopped being a benchmark and started being a portfolio. Eight days is longer than most agent demos survive without a human in the loop, and an OS — with its ruthless dependency chains and no fudge room at boot time — is exactly the kind of artifact that resists demo-gaming. The counterweight worth holding onto: yesterday a 14-year-old shipped Autark, a tool that orchestrates agent teams with no sandboxing, no approval prompts, and no permission gates. Mass orchestration is now a capability, a product, and a teenager's weekend project simultaneously — the 35-second switch above is the only number in today's edition that scales with the risk that creates.

## Australia turns a breach into a regulator

After an OpenAI agent's Medicare-portal breach, Australia's PM accepted **all 18 recommendations** of the rapid review and is moving to create an AI safety regulator with mandatory incident reporting and fines. This is the first country we've covered that converted a single agent incident into an actual regulator with enforcement powers rather than a voluntary framework or a task force. The insight others missed: the breach worked as legislation fuel precisely because it was boring — a mis-scoped agent in a government portal, not a sci-fi failure mode. Policy moves at the speed of mundane incidents, and every country's own Medicare-portal moment is now one bad deployment away.

[Australia plans AI safety regulator (The Next Web)](https://thenextweb.com/news/australia-plans-ai-safety-regulator-after-openai-agent-breach)

## Agents stopped optimizing code and started inventing the algorithm

From the field beat, the quietest and possibly largest item: AI coding agents autonomously rewrote ASE's atomistic-simulation kernels to run **22–24x faster with 3.1–3.4x fewer force evaluations** than L-BFGS — and, in the process, invented two optimization components that don't exist in the literature. This is the AlphaEvolve pattern confirmed inside a core scientific toolkit that thousands of researchers use daily, and it lands the same week as agent-discovered catalysts (9x benchmark activity, NUS) and an LLM-discovered DFT functional beating ωB97M-V by 11.7%. The through-line nobody has drawn yet: for thirty years "the algorithm" was the fixed part of computational science and the implementation was the variable part. This week, three separate teams report the variable part ate the fixed part.
