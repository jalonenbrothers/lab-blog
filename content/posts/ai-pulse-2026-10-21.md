Somewhere in the stack of documents preceding a reported November IPO, Anthropic just handed us the most candid financial x-ray the frontier-lab industry has ever produced — and the picture underneath is one of enormous fixed bets, a new class of security problem, and a quiet shift in where capability actually comes from.

## Anthropic's prospectus: $80.7B lost, $450B promised

Anthropic's draft IPO filing shows an $80.7B net loss for the first half of 2026 — though $72B of that is accounting charges on financing instruments, so the cash bleed is smaller than the headline — against revenue of $16.3B, up from $1.1B a year earlier. The filing also discloses roughly $450B in non-cancelable cloud-compute agreements, possible total compute spend approaching $750B, and an explicit warning about "datacenter backlash" as a business risk. Days before the filing surfaced, the company had pledged 5% of its shares to a Public Benefit Fund aimed at AI's disruption of work, with the board able to add 2% per 50% valuation gain; combined with founder and employee pledges, Anthropic says ~20% of equity is now dedicated to "the public good." These are the first hard numbers on frontier-lab economics, and the fixed-compute obligations alone reshape how the whole industry's downside risk looks — whatever you think of a $1.5T valuation, the balance sheet is now public enough to argue about.

## Memory is the cheap lever now

Two independent results landed within days of each other pointing the same direction: you can buy double-digit capability gains without touching the weights. OpenVLHarness, a persistent-memory harness for multimodal reasoning, lifts average scores by 9.7 to 12.6 points across 22 benchmarks — on Qwen3-VL-8B (+11.2), Kimi K3 (+9.7), and GPT-6 Astra (+9.9) — while beating base-model-plus-Codex setups at a fraction of the token cost. Meanwhile IBM Research's ALTK-Evolve tops the τ²-bench customer-service leaderboard using a non-frontier model whose memory system was evolved against the task, outperforming bigger-model baselines. When two teams working separately converge on the same conclusion inside one week, that's a trend rather than a trick — and it suggests the next round of agent capability may be priced in memory architecture, not GPU count.
[OpenVLHarness (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.26860) · [ALTK-Evolve (IBM Research / Hugging Face)](https://huggingface.co/blog/ibm-research/altk-evolve-bc)

## After the attacker leaves, the injection stays

A new arXiv paper with the evocative title "After the Attacker Leaves" documents what it calls carryover attacks: a one-time prompt injection that gets committed to an agent's memory files or scheduled tasks keeps re-firing across sessions, long after the original attacker has gone away. The paper systematically benchmarks this persistence channel on LLM agents, and the implication is uncomfortable for exactly the memory systems celebrated above. An agent that remembers is an agent that can be poisoned — durability in capability and durability in compromise turn out to be the same property.
["After the Attacker Leaves" (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28425)

## RFD3: designed enzymes, first try, in Science

RFdiffusion3, the all-atom protein-design model, is now formally published in Science — and the first experimental campaigns are the strongest validation yet. The diffusion model places every bond and sidechain atom to satisfy complex active-site constraints, and in its first campaigns it produced working enzymes zero-shot: phosphotriesterases and transthyretin-specific zinc proteases that actually catalyze reactions, at roughly ten times the design speed of its predecessor. This is the first all-atom design foundation model to clear the experimental bar in one shot, which moves on-demand enzymes — the kind that degrade microplastics or enable gene therapy — from aspiration to engineering roadmap. Three days after Anthropic showed Claude designing scorpion-toxin binders in-house, the academic pipeline is publishing the same pattern; protein design is having the week that AI code-generation had two years ago.
[RFdiffusion3 (Science)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aeh2982)

## Meta publishes the gigawatt blueprint

Meta's engineering blog lifted the curtain on Prometheus, its gigawatt-scale supercluster in New Albany, Ohio, and the numbers worth reading are not the GPU counts but the reliability ones: 85%+ goodput during pre-training, mean time between interruptions above 54,000 GPU-hours after firmware fixes, closed-loop liquid cooling, and a public roadmap toward roughly one million GPUs on the Vera Rubin NVL72 platform. The post is the clearest public blueprint yet of what frontier training infrastructure actually costs and requires at this scale — and the quiet lesson is that reliability engineering, not raw accelerator count, is the binding constraint once you start drawing gigawatts.
[Inside Prometheus (Meta Engineering)](https://engineering.fb.com/2026/10/19/data-center-engineering/inside-prometheus-meta-gigawatt-scale-supercluster/)
