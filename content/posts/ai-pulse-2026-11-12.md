## A Maya site found by an agent swarm — and disputed in real time

Pietro Miotti's "My AI Agents (Re)discovered a Maya Site in 72 Hours" hit HN #2 today (968 points, 354 comments), and the split-screen it produced is the story: one side celebrates a jungle feature flagged by cross-referencing public LiDAR and archaeology data, while commenters with remote-sensing backgrounds argue it could be a known site, or a lidar artifact. The insight worth keeping isn't whether this particular rectangle is a ruin — it's that the discovery-and-argument happened in the same thread, on the same day, at hobbyist scale. Archaeology has spent a century requiring expeditions to settle exactly this kind of dispute; the unit of "expedition" just dropped to one person and a weekend of compute.

## Claude planned Perseverance's 400-meter drive — the first AI-planned route on another planet

Anthropic and NASA JPL say Claude helped plan a 400-meter Perseverance traverse on Mars, the first AI-planned rover drive off Earth. The framing almost everyone will miss: this isn't a demo of cleverness, it's a demo of *necessity*. Light-lag to Mars runs 4 to 24 minutes, so a rover that must wait for human-approved waypoints spends most of its life idling; autonomy is the only way to spend that $2.7 billion hardware budget at anything near capacity. The same argument is quietly forming about Earthly agents — any agentic system that parks waiting for human sign-off is burning its own runtime — but Mars makes the case without an office-politics debate.

## AlphaGenome 2: the AlphaFold line moves from structure to meaning

DeepMind released AlphaGenome 2, which tackles how cells read DNA — the regulatory layer where a single-letter variant becomes a disease mechanism, and where sequencing has always outpaced interpretation. The lineage is the story: AlphaFold cracked structure and won a Nobel-adjacent victory lap; AlphaGenome is the succession attempt at the harder half, function. The honest caveat is that "deciphering how cells read DNA" is a benchmark claim today, not a clinic-ready tool, and the field has a long history of models that ace regulatory prediction while clinicians shrug. But if the AlphaFold playbook repeats — messy, over-hyped model → community grumbles → structure becomes infrastructure — genome interpretation just got its 2020 moment.

## NTT's robots rehearse the lift before doing it

NTT's physical-AI release bolts a physics-control adapter onto a video-generation world model so a robot can *simulate* a candidate action — mass, orientation, load shift — before moving a finger, lifting shelf-stacking success from 73.1% to 92.3%, with airport baggage trials alongside Mujin planned. The angle: the robotics discourse keeps fixating on dexterity, but dexterity is wasted on a robot that grabs wrong. "Predict, then act" is the unglamorous pattern that makes automation insurable enough for messy unstructured loads — baggage handling is a domain where a dropped suitcase is a complaint and a crushed one is a lawsuit. If 2025 was the year of the demo, the quiet pattern to watch for 2027 is robots that visibly pause to think.

[NTT release](https://group.ntt/en/newsrelease/2026/11/11/261111b.html)

## Synthetic data cuts the cost of asking "what if we did?"

MIT today published a technique for training climate emulators on a fraction of the usual data: an algorithm generates a tailored synthetic dataset of optimized climate scenarios, and the resulting emulator captures local pollutant-policy tradeoffs (cut one pollutant, another rises) that traditional emulators miss — in seconds. What it doesn't say: this attacks the cost bottleneck, not the uncertainty bottleneck. A thousand cheap scenarios are only as honest as the synthetic data beneath them, and the history of emulators is a history of confidently fast wrong answers. The 50-year view, though, is that resilience planning needs *thousands* of policy sweeps that full Earth-system models can never cheaply provide — this makes them cheap, and lets other people argue about whether they're true.
