Eighty percent of a doubling, and the receipts are classified. That is the strangest shape of today's lead story: for the first time, the recursive loop everyone theorizes about — AI making better AI — has been measured from inside a frontier lab, and the most persuasive evidence for the measurement is one you will never see.

## Anthropic's R&D is running ~2x pace, and AI gets the credit

METR, the evaluation shop with a track record of deflating hype, published a high-access assessment (Nov 5) finding that Anthropic's internal AI R&D is moving at roughly twice its 2024-25 pace, with ~80% of the speedup attributed to AI-assisted research — and no clear bottleneck yet in sight. The catch is structural, not incidental: the strongest evidence is redacted, so the headline number is verifiable only in the sense that METR is trustworthy.

Why it matters, and the angle nobody is drawing: DeepMind's own audited self-assessment (which we covered Oct 16) concluded there was *no significant* end-to-end acceleration of AI R&D. Two professionally audited labs, opposite verdicts, five months apart. That disagreement is more informative than either number — it suggests the loop is real but unevenly litigated, and that "trust the auditor" is quietly becoming the load-bearing wall of AI policy. Regulation built on redacted assessments is regulation built on reputation.

[Read the METR assessment](https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-05-anthropic-ai-rd-acceleration-assessment/)

## An AI-designed vaccine that covers flu variants that don't exist yet

Cambridge researchers (with spin-out DIOSynVax) used evolutionary algorithms on global surveillance sequence data to design five synthetic antigens spanning bird-flu diversity — a vaccine for a family, not a strain. In mouse and ferret trials it fully protected the animals where the WHO stockpile vaccine left only 50% alive (Cell Reports Medicine, Nov 6).

The interesting shift is in what a government buys. Pandemic stockpiling has always been a bet on guessing next year's strain; this turns the procurement unit into *coverage of the family*, including members that haven't emerged. It's the same team whose AI-designed coronavirus vaccine already passed early safety trials — this is their third designed pathogen, which makes it look less like a stunt and more like a pipeline.

## Meta declares memory the frontier metric

Meta rolled out specialized models, distilled from its Muse Wonder flagship, across Muse and Meta AI (Nov 6) — and the numbers it chose to lead with say everything: a "Personal Superintelligence Index" up 47% vs 38%, Active Memory at 90% vs 76% with a 2.4x reduction in forgetting critical information, and +67% on everyday tasks with unexpected obstacles.

The quiet move here is metric design. When a lab invents an index, it is telling you where it thinks the race will be won — and Meta is betting the consumer frontier is persistence, not puzzle-solving. There's also a commodity squeeze hiding in the announcement: distilling frontier capability down to free-tier assistants erodes the paid chatbot's differentiation, while memory (the accumulated context that is painful to rebuild elsewhere) is precisely the thing that creates lock-in. Give away the intelligence, monetize the remembering.

[Meta's announcement](https://research.meta.ai/blog/specialized-models-for-muse-and-metaai)

## Robots learned from 16,151 hours of humans holding a gripper

AIRoA-0, a robot foundation model announced Nov 6, was trained purely on human manipulation data recorded with YUBI, a handheld gripper — no teleoperation, no robots, no RL in the data pipeline. It averages 78% success across ten bimanual tasks against 27% for the best rival VLA, and transfers zero-shot to an unseen site and four robot platforms (Franka, TX-G2, OpenArm, UR).

The contrarian read: the field's expensive answer to the robot-data bottleneck has been teleop farms and simulation at scale, and this result suggests the better answer was to leave the robot out of data collection entirely. A handheld gripper costs a rounding error next to a robot fleet, humans are plentiful, and 16K hours of recordings beat the teleop-trained incumbents three-to-one. Watch for a wave of copycat human-data collection schemes — the barrier to entry in robot learning may have just dropped from capital to patience.

[AIRoA-0 announcement](https://airoa.org/updates/20261106/832/)

## The "AI scientist" is now a download

Deep Principle released Hydrogen-1.0 (Nov 6), claiming the first general-purpose open-weight scientific-discovery model: GLM-5.3 post-trained on lab records and agent trajectories, MIT-licensed, 1M-token context — and reportedly beating GPT-6 Astra on FrontierScience-Research while roughly matching Kimi K3 on GPQA-Diamond.

The significance isn't the leaderboard line; it's the provenance shift. Every previous "AI scientist" claim lived in a press release and a demo video you couldn't audit. This one lives in weights you can download, run, and attempt to replicate — and because it rides on an open base model, the entire stack is inspectable. Claims about AI-driven science become falsifiable in the way science claims are supposed to be, which is a bigger deal than any single benchmark score.
