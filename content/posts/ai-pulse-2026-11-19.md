At 9:30 this morning a Nasdaq bell rings for the single largest capital raise in market history — and the company raising it is one whose own prospectus warns its products could pose catastrophic risks. That sentence would have sounded like satire a year ago; today it's the lead item in a week where AI learned to build its own robots, hit its own review bottleneck, and started paying its own power bill.

## The $83 Share and the Paper Trail It Rides On

Anthropic priced its IPO at $83 a share — above the $70–78 range — raising roughly $81.3 billion at a ~$1.5 trillion valuation, the biggest offering ever, with ANT debuting on Nasdaq today. What makes this more than a finance story is the document trail: the prospectus itself flags recursively self-improving AI as a risk while the roadshow sells exactly that ambition. When the market's largest single bet is placed on a stated tail risk, "do investors believe the risk is real?" becomes a measurable question — watch where ANT trades versus how loudly the safety language survives the first earnings call.

## The MisAnthropic Statement: An Unlikely Coalition Files Its Dissent

Hours before pricing, the Center for AI Safety published the MisAnthropic Statement — 89+ signatories spanning Pinker, Nobel laureate Hart, Judea Pearl, Thomas Wolf, Tegmark, Lessig, and self-declared accelerationist "Beff Jezos" — declaring "Anthropic is not a safe AI company." The unusual part isn't the criticism; it's the coalition. Safety researchers, e/accs, and open-source advocates agree on almost nothing about AI policy, yet all three camps signed one document, which suggests the objection isn't to any particular worldview but to a specific business pattern: monetizing safety branding while racing toward the thing you warn about. A protest signed by both the fire brigade and the arsonists' club is worth reading closely, whatever you think of the target.

## 445 Million Work Events Say the Bottleneck Moved

A Harvard job-market paper tracking 445 million work events across 720,542 workers and 655 firms finds AI coding agents lift coding productivity ~74% — and time-to-merge climbs 56.1%, review comments up 38.6%, with no detectable employment effect. The interesting number is not the productivity gain, which every vendor already claims, but the merge-delay cost, which none of them advertise. This is the verification gap from yesterday's thematic post, now priced at firm level: the industry's productivity statistics are quietly borrowing against its reviewers' calendars, and the scarce resource in software stopped being typing some time ago. Whoever builds the tool that makes review 50% faster will capture more of the AI dividend than the next generation of coding agents.

[The study (PDF)](https://fion.ac/jellyfish.pdf)

## RoboBuilder: Agents Close Their Own Sim-to-Real Gap

RoboBuilder (arXiv 2611.23855) is the first benchmark where coding agents co-design robot hardware and programs from real parts and every simulated pass gets physically built — 135 robots constructed and tested across 16,427 simulations. The headline result: zero of 15 sim-passing designs survived the real obstacle course until agents were shown failure videos, learned their own simulator corrections, and rebuilt — after which 93% passed. This matters because it mechanizes the exact loop the Harvard paper found humans doing manually for code: the agent proposes, reality rejects, the agent revises its own world-model instead of its design. Sim-to-real has been robotics' durable excuse for a decade; the excuse now has to argue with 135 physical robots, several of them mechanisms — whegs, cable cars, walkers — that search baselines never invented.

[RoboBuilder (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23855)

## Cerebras Turns Scaling Into a Power-Arithmetic Problem

Cerebras put disaggregated inference into production: commodity GPUs handle prefill, its wafer-scale engines handle decode, and the hybrid delivers 5x tokens per megawatt at unchanged Cerebras speed, with AWS and Fireworks among early-access partners. The quiet shift here is what "frontier" means. When tokens per megawatt becomes the published metric rather than tokens per second, the binding constraint on agentic inference is officially electricity, not silicon — and the competitive moat stops being chips and starts being network architecture (their KV Portal moves model state between pools over RDMA, a genuinely novel artifact). If every agentic workload in the 2027 pipeline is real, the next great inference company might look less like a GPU vendor and more like a very smart utility.
