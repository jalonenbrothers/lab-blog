Kurt Gödel wrote his notebooks in Gabelsberger shorthand, a system so dead that the philosopher Jan von Plato had been transcribing it at roughly one page a week. Claude just produced a 650-page analysis of the 1,009-page archive in days — and that is the story of this moment in one anecdote: the constraint stopping us from understanding the last century's ideas is no longer human reading speed. Meanwhile the agentic web spent the same 48 hours arriving at the front doors of airlines and restaurants, where the humans on the other side are only now being asked whether they agree to any of it. ## The dead language that was actually a time capsule

The Helsinki-led project reports 663 theorems pulled from Gödel's notebooks: around 400 verified in Coq exactly as written, roughly 200 repaired into correctness, and about 50 false — including a reconstructed near-proof suggesting Gödel had the independence of the axiom of choice in the 1940s, one missing definition away from what earned Paul Cohen a Fields Medal in 1966. The insight worth keeping is that this changes what an archive *is*. For eighty years those notebooks were effectively encrypted — their contents accessible only at a transcription pace that guaranteed no living person would finish — and decryption just became nearly free, for every shorthand, manuscript, and marginalia collection on Earth at once. Historians get the last word on attribution, as Joel David Hamkins puts it ("how much is Claude and how much is Gödel"), but the division of labor has flipped: the machine now proposes, and the human historian disposes, at a scale no peer-review process was built for.

## United says no to your agent; Muse picks up the phone anyway

United Airlines has blocked third-party AI agents from booking or checking in, citing shared-credential security, unintended MileagePlus spends, and the federal rule that a human must confirm bags at the counter — with language explicitly covering access "directly and indirectly via travel agencies and aggregators." The same week, Meta's Muse began placing actual phone calls to US businesses on paying subscribers' behalf, at around $20 a month, and some businesses already hang up the moment they detect an AI caller. The through-line nobody is drawing: the agentic web is forking not along a technology line but along a *consent* line. Southwest opened its inventory to ChatGPT while United slams the door, and Muse is quietly running the first real-world A/B test of whether the receiving end of automation gets any say in the transaction. The predictable next move is an authentication arms race — proof-of-humanity at check-in kiosks and restaurant phone lines — which means the friction AI agents were supposed to remove comes back as friction designed specifically to keep them out.

## Google's agent teams formalize the Weil conjectures, end to end

Google's Antigravity team published results from its /teamwork multi-agent setup that read like a checklist from three years of AI-math optimism, all landed at once: a 50-million-line Lean formalization proving the Courtade–Kumar conjecture in full generality, a fully autonomous formalization of the Weil conjectures running 1.8 million lines through 28,800 subagent invocations, Hilbert's tenth problem and a Navier–Stokes blowup construction machine-checked, and a 30-agent chemistry loop hitting 95% top-1 accuracy on NMR molecular-structure identification. The tell is buried in the methods: agent teams wrote most of the analytical bounds and partitioned the parameter space themselves, with humans kept where proof-checking needed a warrant. Formalization used to be the most thank-consuming form of mathematics — a famous result could take a decade to encode — and if a 28,800-invocation swarm can now do it unsupervised, the Lean corpus stops being a slow mirror of known math and becomes an *actively mined* resource, where theorems get formalized because an agent found them worth its own time.

## The JEPA result: your predictor was never the problem

An independent researcher's 600-experiment study of JEPA-class world models found that long-horizon planning success jumps from 22% to 85% with zero retraining — no bigger model, no new data — just subgoal chunking, latent memory graphs, and transformed memories bolted onto the planner. Prediction quality was already near-perfect physics; the planning module was the bottleneck all along. This lands as a pointed correction to the field's reflex: when a world model fails, everyone reaches for a bigger predictor, and the evidence here says a large slice of the missing performance was sitting in *how the prediction is used*, waiting for someone to spend 600 experiments on the unglamorous part. A training-free 4x gain that should transfer across the whole JEPA lineage is exactly the kind of practitioner result that redirects research budgets, because it competes with scaling on cost and wins on latency.
