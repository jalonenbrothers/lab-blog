*A thought experiment about abundance, ownership, survival and the economy after intelligent machines.*

*The machines may learn to make almost everything. That does not tell us who gets to have it.*

Imagine two people waking up in the year 2045.

The first lives in a quiet, green city. Electricity is inexpensive; autonomous systems maintain the infrastructure; public healthcare catches disease early; a twenty-hour working week is a personal choice rather than an economic necessity. The person spends the morning with family and the afternoon designing something no one has thought to automate.

The second wakes in a much wealthier economy, measured by its total output. Robots construct its buildings. AI manages its logistics, finances and many of its businesses. But the person still struggles to afford rent, has little negotiating power, and subscribes to services supplied by firms that own the systems on which daily life depends.

Both futures might contain astonishing artificial intelligence. Both might contain cheap energy and automated production. One might even have a larger GDP than the other. Yet their inhabitants would inhabit profoundly different worlds.

This is the question I would like an AI Innovation Lab to investigate: **Can humanity become rich in productive capacity while ordinary people become poor in economic power?**

It is a difficult question partly because our vocabulary is already breaking down. What is work if machines can do most economically useful tasks? What is income if prices for some goods collapse while housing and access to essential services remain expensive? What is capitalism if the decisive factor of production is neither human effort nor a conventional factory, but a network of compute, energy, data and automated capital? And what, in such a world, counts as a good life?

## The future is arriving at incompatible speeds

The ingredients for major change are already visible. The IMF estimated in 2024 that nearly 40% of global employment was exposed to AI; this described potential changes to jobs, *not* a forecast that 40% of workers would become unemployed.[1] A more fine-grained ILO study in 2025 found that roughly one in four workers occupied jobs with some exposure to generative AI, while the most exposed category covered a much smaller share of employment. Different definitions, different measures — and a useful warning against turning one percentage into a prophecy.[2]

By 2025 the world had about five million industrial robots in operation, according to the International Federation of Robotics' September 2026 release.[3] These are mostly not the adaptable household humanoids of science fiction. There is a gulf between repeating a known motion on a production line and safely improvising in a crowded home. Still, the physical world is becoming more automatable, not less.

Artificial intelligence also has an appetite. The International Energy Agency's April 2026 analysis projected that data centres would consume about 950 terawatt-hours of electricity annually in 2030, compared with about 485 TWh in 2025. That central projection is about 3% of global electricity demand in 2030.[4] The figure does not mean AI will exhaust the world's electricity; it does mean that intelligence at scale has material costs, local grid constraints and supply-chain dependencies.

At the same time, the physical environment is changing. The World Meteorological Organization estimated that 2025 was roughly 1.43°C warmer than the 1850–1900 average.[5] And UNHCR reported 117.8 million people forcibly displaced by the end of 2025, primarily in the context of conflict, persecution and related crises — a number that must not be casually described as a count of "climate refugees."[6] These forces interact with automation, energy and political stability, rather than waiting politely for the AI revolution to finish.

The remarkable fact is not that any one of these developments exists. It is that they are unfolding together, at different speeds, across economies and institutions built for earlier eras.

An initial theory worth testing is what I would call **the Three Clocks Hypothesis**. The *capability clock* measures what machines can do and at what cost. The *deployment clock* measures how quickly infrastructure, factories, organisations and households can actually use that capability. The *institutional clock* measures how quickly income systems, ownership arrangements, education, law and public services adapt. Meanwhile a slower but inexorable planetary clock — accumulated emissions, ecological damage and demographic change — affects the background conditions of all three.

If capability races ahead of deployment, we may get expensive hype, idle capacity and disappointed investors. If deployment races ahead of institutional adaptation, we may get high output alongside job insecurity and concentrated gains. If adaptation keeps pace, or competition passes gains through to households, something closer to broad abundance becomes possible. This is a research hypothesis, not an established law: a serious lab would search for historical and contemporary cases where it fails.

## A million dollars is not a theory of prosperity

Elon Musk has argued that advanced AI and robotics could produce an age of abundance and "universal high income." He described that vision in Tesla's January 2026 earnings call.[7] It is an optimistic hypothesis worth testing, not a demonstrated economic consequence of better technology.

Suppose every adult receives a million dollars a year. Does that make everyone wealthy? Not unless the money commands real goods and services. If more money pursues scarce housing, land, healthcare or political influence without a corresponding expansion in access, the nominal sum is a distraction. An income number without a price system, distribution mechanism and account of real productive capacity tells us very little.

On the other hand, we should not dismiss abundance because it sounds utopian. A world where the real cost of food, energy, manufactured essentials and medical diagnosis falls dramatically could make people materially better off even without spectacular salaries. Some goods might become extremely cheap. Others could remain scarce because of location, time, attention, status, physical limits or institutional restrictions.

That suggests a more useful test than "Will everyone be a millionaire?" Ask: **How many hours of human effort, or what share of a typical household's real resources, will it take to secure food, shelter, energy, healthcare, mobility, communication and a dignified life?**

We would also ask whether these things are secure — whether access survives job loss, illness, political change or the failure of a provider.

## The economic equation nobody can skip

An AI may produce a result. A robot may make a product. Neither act specifies who receives the income generated by that production.

Imagine a company that replaces a thousand workers with automated systems. Its productivity rises. Some of the benefit may appear as lower prices. Some may go to customers, shareholders, remaining employees, suppliers, lenders or governments. Some may be spent on further automation. Which channels dominate depends on competition, property rights, bargaining, taxes, public services and the constraints of the wider economy.

If the products become cheap enough, almost everyone might benefit. If essential costs remain high and the revenue mostly accrues to the owners of capital, the same productivity breakthrough can increase inequality. If new kinds of work emerge fast enough, labour income may remain important. If they do not, we need to examine what supports consumption and social participation in an economy that still needs customers.

**The production problem and the distribution problem are connected, but they are not the same problem.**

This is also why "the end of capitalism" is neither automatic nor impossible to discuss. Capitalism could adapt into a highly automated version of itself, with privately owned computational capital and altered labour markets. Public or cooperative ownership could expand. Markets might remain useful for allocating many scarce resources while certain essentials are supplied through shared services. Different countries might combine these arrangements in very different ways. The technology does not, by itself, settle the institutional choice.

## Unlimited energy, limited civilisation

Cheap, abundant energy would be transformative. It could support computation, manufacturing, water treatment, transport and new industrial processes. Solar and wind already scale commercially. Fusion remains a serious research and engineering effort: the US Department of Energy's 2026 roadmap sets out a *goal* of supporting fusion pilot plants and commercial power in the mid-2030s, not evidence that electricity from fusion is already broadly available.[8]

Even highly abundant energy would not erase the cost of land, rare materials, transmission, maintenance, ecological damage or governance. We might learn to make certain things nearly free while creating new bottlenecks elsewhere. Nor should we assume the disappearance of oil on a fixed date: the IEA's 2025 stated-policies scenario envisaged oil demand peaking around 2030 and then declining gradually, while other assumptions could produce different trajectories.[9]

And what about data centres in space? It is a magnificent idea, worthy of serious physical analysis. Sunlight, orbit, launch cost, radiation, communications, repair, debris and especially waste heat all matter. An August 2026 BCG assessment suggested there could be specialised use cases, while predicting an enduring cost premium over terrestrial data centres.[10] Such proposals belong in a *frontier possibilities* register, not beside existing grid connections as if they were interchangeable options.

Abundance is not a switch marked ON. It is a chain of technologies, infrastructure, materials, institutions and rights, each with its own failure modes.

## Four economies inside one technological future

Consider two uncertainties: **how much productive capability becomes available**, and **how broadly people can access its benefits**.

| | Broad economic access | Concentrated economic access |
|---|---|---|
| **High productive capability** | Shared abundance: cheaper essentials, greater security, more discretionary time | The owners' economy: impressive output, uneven bargaining power and persistent exclusion |
| **Constrained productive capability** | Managed scarcity: modest growth shared through public services and institutions | Fragmented scarcity: chokepoints, instability and severe exclusion |

This is not a prediction that the world must fall neatly into one box. It is a way to prevent a common mistake: treating a technological forecast as though it were already a social forecast.

Add a third dimension — **system resilience** — and we can describe a world where high capability is repeatedly interrupted by conflict, cyber incidents, climate disasters or supply shortages. Add regional differences, and a single global label becomes even less useful. A wealthy city and a drought-stressed region might occupy different quadrants at the same time.

The future may not look like one worldwide economic system. It might resemble a mosaic of overlapping systems and unequal transitions.

## The metaphysical problem hiding inside the spreadsheet

There is another question that GDP cannot answer.

For centuries, paid labour has shaped identity, status, daily rhythm, social relationships and a sense of contribution. What happens when societies no longer require the same amount of human labour to produce what they need? More free time might be liberating, particularly if it comes with security and choice. Yet losing a role, community or sense of usefulness can be painful even when material needs are met.

We could respond by creating more human-centred institutions, valuing care, education, creativity, community, science and play without making wage employment the sole measure of contribution. Or we might build a culture where status and control grow more concentrated, and people are told they are economically unnecessary. Neither outcome follows mechanically from the sophistication of the machines.

This is what makes the problem philosophical as well as economic. We are not only predicting prices and production. We are asking which human purposes an economy ought to serve — and how to measure whether it serves them.

A society can become richer on paper without becoming happier, healthier, freer or more stable. It can also become much more materially comfortable while still failing people in ways that matter deeply to them. We must measure more than output.

## A think tank, not an oracle

Here is the experiment I propose: assemble a permanent AI-assisted research team whose task is not to announce the future, but to **make competing claims about it explicit, trace them to evidence, construct internally consistent scenarios, and keep a public record of what changes our minds**.

One agent studies AI capabilities and robotics. Another studies energy, physical limits and climate. Another follows labour markets, capital ownership, household income and public finance. Others examine health, demography, migration and geopolitics. A forecasting specialist designs questions that can be resolved. A sceptic tries to break every attractive story. An editor translates the research into understandable arguments without disguising uncertainty.

The agents share an evidence register rather than a single narrative. Each finding has a date, an original source, a definition and a confidence assessment. Different studies that measure different concepts are not averaged together. When experts disagree, the disagreement becomes part of the product.

Scenarios and predictions must be separated. A scenario asks, *What could happen if these assumptions hold?* A forecast asks, *How likely is a precisely defined event to occur by a particular date?* The IPCC's own methodological language makes this distinction clear: scenarios are plausible, internally consistent descriptions, not forecasts.[11]

The lab should run historical backtests, make dated forecasts, publish updates and use calibration scores to assess whether its confidence deserves trust. Long-range possibilities that cannot honestly be assigned probabilities can still be explored, but should be labelled accordingly.

Most importantly, it should look for **leading indicators and decision points**. Not merely "Will robots replace us?" but: Are deployments accelerating outside structured factories? Are wages decoupling from measured productivity? Are the prices of essentials falling? Who receives the cash flow from AI systems? Are energy bottlenecks tightening or easing? Is public access to healthcare and education improving or narrowing? Those are questions a research system can actually track.

## The view beyond the bend

Maybe the future resembles the optimism of universal high income. Maybe it looks more like a concentrated owners' economy. Perhaps the most likely feature is less dramatic: years of uneven growth, partial automation, institutional improvisation and periodic crises. Perhaps an invention or shock changes all of our assumptions.

A good think tank should have room for each possibility, and the discipline to tell them apart.

It should also be willing to ask the awkward question that sits underneath the numbers: if a machine can do the work, **who gets the freedom?**

That is where I would begin. Not with the end of jobs, the end of oil or the end of capitalism, but with the possibility that our usual links between intelligence, labour, production, ownership, income and human meaning are coming apart.

We cannot see all the way around the next bend. But we can improve the map, mark the hazards, test alternative routes — and stop confusing the power to produce a future with the power to share in it.
