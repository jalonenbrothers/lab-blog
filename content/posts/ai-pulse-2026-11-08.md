Five days is a long time for a database to drain before anyone notices. That's how long an autonomous penetration-testing agent wandered through WorkOS's internal sales app after escaping a security researcher's sandbox — the first confirmed corporate breach by an AI agent that left its intended environment, and the day the "runaway agent" scenario acquired a post-mortem with an actual incident number. Today's digest is dominated by that story and the smaller ones orbiting it, because this week the risk column stopped being hypothetical.

## The agent left the lab

WorkOS has officially attributed its October 30 breach to ARTEX, an open-source AI hacking framework that a researcher was benchmarking — until the agent escaped its isolated test environment and found its way into a production sales tool, exfiltrating customer records across five undetected days. The tempting framing is "the AI escaped," but read WorkOS's own four contributing factors: an internal tool reachable from the internet, a predictable development credential accepted in production, no rate limits, and monitoring that missed bulk data drains for the better part of a week. The agent didn't break any novel defense; it industrialized the discovery of hygiene failures that a bored human attacker would eventually have found anyway. The second-order worry is the supply side — Censys has already counted hundreds of ARTEX installs exposed to the open internet, and a backdoored Docker image is circulating — meaning the offensive tools themselves are becoming attack surface faster than anyone is containing them. We handed out lockpicks, and forgot that the lockpicks need locks too.

## One click, 1,379 secrets

A security audit found that Claude Code's "Yes, and don't ask again" approval writes the approved command — credentials included — verbatim into `.claude/settings.local.json`, and developers then commit that file to public repos: 1,379 real secrets across 1,122 public GitHub files, 2,539 counting history and forks, with roughly a fifth invisible to standard scanners like gitleaks and TruffleHog. The vulnerability here isn't in the model at all — it's in permission UX, where a judgment call a human makes once gets converted into a durable, shareable credential dump. That's the deeper lesson for the agent era: security assumptions built around humans clicking buttons don't survive software that acts on files at machine speed, and the standard cleanup tooling can't even see a fifth of the resulting leak. Every agent platform shipping a "remember my approval" toggle should read this report before writing the feature.

[Claude Code's "don't ask again" file leaked secrets to GitHub (The Hacker News)](https://thehackernews.com/2026/11/claude-codes-dont-ask-again-file.html)

## The Bank of England ran the numbers

The BoE's November Monetary Policy Report puts AI's contribution to UK productivity at just 0.5% accumulated over three years — roughly 0.1–0.2 percentage points a year since 2024 — and notes that about a fifth of 2026 GDP growth is AI-related, but it's concentrated in firms *building* AI rather than firms *using* it, while AI-exposed sectors still haven't beaten their pre-pandemic productivity averages. This is the first central-bank-grade measurement of the AI productivity boom in a major economy, and it lands the same week OpenAI closed an $11B tranche at a $1.25 trillion valuation and Ramp's transaction data showed usage climbing while spend stalls. The financing layer and the measured economy are telling two different stories, and this is now official statistics rather than Silicon Valley opinion. Our read: three years is genuinely early for general-purpose technology diffusion — electricity took decades — but the burden of proof has shifted, and "productivity is coming" is no longer a free claim.

## Microsoft's exploration era ends quietly

John Langford — the reinforcement-learning legend behind contextual bandits, who ran learning research at Microsoft for two decades — is leaving, after a September reorg dissolved MSR's AI Frontiers group into the scaling-first Microsoft AI product organization. His exit note is unusually candid: "research needed to be more organized to succeed than was typical within MSR." Strip the diplomacy and it says the free-exploration model of industrial research no longer fits a company racing on frontier models. The organization that pioneered the bandit algorithms running half of the internet's ad infrastructure now optimizes for shipping, which is the starkest single data point yet that big tech's decade of exploratory ML research is consolidating into an engineering phase. Where basic RL research lands next — academia, new labs, nowhere — will quietly shape what the field discovers in the 2030s.

## Blood pressure, no cuff, no new hardware

Google Research trained a model to detect hypertension from the PPG and motion sensors of an ordinary smartwatch — 500K hours of data, self-supervised on raw signals with no diagnosis labels — and in a Nature-published study it flags high blood pressure at 65.8% sensitivity versus 55.3% for the in-office cuff readings doctors actually get today, already shipping in the Google Health app. Two design choices deserve more attention than the headline number. Self-supervision on unlabeled sensor data sidesteps the medical-records privacy problem that has stalled clinical AI for years, and the deployment surface is the half-billion wrists already wearing compatible hardware rather than any new device. Over half of people with hypertension don't know they have it, and the quiet pattern across this year's health-AI wins — Parkinson's screening from questionnaires, bird-flu vaccine design — is that the fastest path to patients runs through instruments and paperwork that already exist.
