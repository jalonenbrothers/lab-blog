Eleven days. That's how long the world's most valuable AI-supply company spent without the founder who personifies it — Nvidia's board reinstated Jensen Huang as CEO today, walked back an espionage-adjacent governance crisis, and watched the stock rise 33% on the news ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/23/nvidia-jensen-huang-ceo.html)). The whole affair compresses the arc of AI-era governance into a single fortnight, and it sits alongside a day that also gave us a new frontier architecture, a slowing-down proposal from inside the fastest lab, and an agent that weaponizes software patches.

## Nvidia blinks first, or maybe politics does

The board that opened an investigation into Huang's alleged Chinese-spy relationship on November 8 reinstated him on November 23, with Senate testimony still scheduled for next week and the President reportedly calling into the board meeting to say there was "no evidence of wrongdoing" ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/23/nvidia-jensen-huang-ceo.html), corroborated by [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/23/nvidia-reverses-course-and-brings-jensen-huang-back-as-ceo/)). The read nobody is drawing: the market treated the removal of the person nominally under suspicion as the risk, not the suspicion itself — a 33% pop says investors price Nvidia's leadership as infrastructure, the way they'd price a downed power grid. Once a CEO is load-bearing for a strategic industry, "under investigation" stops being a governance state and becomes a supply constraint, and the political system has now demonstrated it will intervene to remove the constraint before any process concludes. That precedent — executive pressure resolving a board-level national-security review in eleven days — will outlast Huang's tenure, whatever the Senate hears next week.

## GLM-5.4: the frontier gains a third axis

Z.ai shipped GLM-5.4, its first loop-transformer model: a shared block of layers executed across multiple iterations, with loop depth and size scaled in parallel, aimed at generalized coding ([Z.ai](https://z.ai/blog/glm-5.4)). While the Western frontier argues about parameter counts and data walls, Z.ai scaled compute-per-token by making the model think in circles — recurrent depth moved from research curiosity to a production frontier release in one step, and it grabbed the day's top AI story on Hacker News to prove the market noticed. The deeper point is architectural: for two years "frontier" has meant roughly one design, so labs competed on a single axis and benchmark progress measured everything. A genuinely different architecture that competes head-on breaks the assumption that capability is a one-dimensional race — and with it, the assumption that whoever spends the most on the biggest cluster necessarily wins.

## OpenAI's chief scientist proposes a speed limit, in the industry's own units

Jakub Pachocki published internal numbers showing perplexity on the reference corpus falling from roughly 0.99 (GPT-3.5) to below 0.8 today, and proposed perplexity-based industry pacing limits plus shared red-team norms — while in the same post predicting an AI-driven "avalanche" of pretraining advances as early as 2027 ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/perplexity-and-intelligence/)). The contrarian-but-defensible take is that this is not a safety gesture at all: perplexity is the one capability metric that outsiders can audit from a model's outputs, while every benchmarks-vs-real-world dispute this year has hinged on numbers only the lab can see. If pacing limits attach to an auditable quantity, the industry gains a common ruler for the first time — and the lab that's ahead on that ruler gets to define "too fast" for everyone else. Whether that's restraint or a moat drawn in loss units is exactly the question the open-weights camp (loudly, and personally, today) is asking.

## Google's exploit_agent: patches are now attack intel

Google's Threat Intelligence Group documented what it calls a critical inflection point: a multi-agent LLM framework, exploit_agent, that reads a security patch's diff, drafts a working exploit, and fires it at exposed systems with no human in the loop — and they found it live on an attacker's staging server ([Google Cloud blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/agentic-threat-activity-crosses-a-significant-threshold)). The part worth sitting with is the economics: patch diffs are public, structured, and describe exactly one thing — where the bug is — which makes patched vulnerabilities the *more* efficient target for a machine that reads faster than defenders can deploy. Zero-day hunting used to be the prestige attack because finding the bug was the expensive part; exploit_agent inverts that, so every Patch Tuesday is now a machine-readable vulnerability disclosure to whoever builds the better reader. The patch window, the one thing every security regime on Earth depends on, just got bounded by machine time rather than analyst time.

## A robot learned to move without ever watching a human do it

An MIT/Berkeley/NVIDIA team trained an open 27B reasoning model to drive real dual-arm robots using pure reinforcement learning in simulation — no teleoperation demonstrations, the data collection method that has been the field's bottleneck and moat for a decade — lifting LIBERO scores from 1.6% to 74.4% and succeeding on 18 of 30 real-hardware trials ([arXiv 2611.18562](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18562)). The 18-of-30 number is the honest caveat and the story at once: transfer from simulation to metal is still lossy, but the training recipe is now the same one that produced computer-use agents — chain-of-thought in, joint motor commands out, learned entirely in an environment you can reset a million times. Robotics just stopped being a data-collection race and became a simulation-and-compute race, which is a race Nvidia, Google, and every frontier lab is already equipped to run. The teleop datasets everyone spent fortunes recording are now worth roughly what labeled ImageNet was after self-supervision landed.
