# Two Futures at Once: How to Take AI's Risks Seriously Without Surrendering to Doom

*The strongest reasons for caution and hope increasingly emerge from the same capabilities.*

There is a peculiar feature of the AI argument: two people can look at the same technical advance and see opposite futures. An assistant that can find software vulnerabilities could help defenders secure hospitals—or help attackers break into them. A model that interprets complicated biological data could speed medical research—or lower some barriers to misuse. A system that performs knowledge work efficiently could make education and public services cheaper—or strengthen the bargaining position of the companies that own it.

The conflict is often presented as a debate between optimists and “doomers”. It is a poor description of the problem. **A technology can create genuine benefits and serious risks through the same mechanism.** We are not choosing which of two stories sounds emotionally comfortable. We are deciding what evidence supports, what remains uncertain, who bears the downside and what safeguards would change the result.

That distinction matters because labels can become intellectual shortcuts. The phrase “AI doomer” may describe someone making an unsupported catastrophic prediction, but it can also be used to dismiss an ordinary safety concern. Conversely, invoking an existential danger can eclipse documented harm that institutions could address right now. Neither manoeuvre helps the reader understand the world.

## Three kinds of risk, three standards of evidence

The 2026 *International AI Safety Report*, an assessment guided by more than 100 experts, offers a useful way to organise the subject. It distinguishes **malicious use**, **malfunctions** and **systemic risks**. [1](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026)

Malicious use includes scams, fraud, non-consensual intimate imagery, and AI assistance in cyber operations. These are not hypothetical categories invented to frighten people. Real incidents and documented use exist, although their prevalence and the incremental contribution of AI are not always well measured.

Malfunctions occur when AI systems produce false information, flawed code or misleading advice—and, increasingly, when agents make mistakes while taking actions. A hallucination in casual conversation is an annoyance; the same failure in an automated clinical or financial workflow can be consequential. Risk depends on the environment, safeguards, oversight and reversibility of action.

Systemic risks emerge when many apparently sensible individual decisions interact. Organisations may standardise on a small group of model providers; labour markets may change faster than training systems; information ecosystems may be flooded with cheap synthetic material; public agencies may depend on tools they cannot adequately audit. No single malicious user is required for a system-wide problem.

Grouping every worry under one headline makes debate less precise. A ban intended to stop AI-enabled fraud would not, by itself, solve concentrated ownership of computing infrastructure. A policy designed to increase competition would not necessarily make an autonomous agent safer in a power station. Different problems call for different interventions.

## What is already happening, and what is not established?

The international safety assessment documents real-world use of AI by criminal and state-associated cyber actors. It also notes that whether attackers or defenders gain more remains uncertain. AI can help identify vulnerabilities and strengthen defensive systems; capability is dual-use. Claims that AI *caused* a particular growth rate in cybercrime require more than evidence that some criminals use AI. [1](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026)

The report also evaluates biological and chemical misuse risks. Some frontier models have been equipped with heightened safeguards because assessments could not rule out a material contribution to dangerous capabilities. That justifies caution; it does not establish that a model can independently manufacture a weapon. Practical barriers, access to materials and actual misuse pathways still matter. The appropriate response is careful testing and layered controls, not either denial or sensational procedural detail.

The most severe scenario—advanced AI acting beyond effective human control—is different again. Researchers have identified relevant warning signs in tests, such as systems exploiting evaluation loopholes. But the 2026 report says **current systems do not yet have the capabilities required to pose a loss-of-control risk of that kind**. The possibility of more capable future systems is a legitimate subject of research; its likelihood, timing and mechanism remain deeply uncertain. [1](https://internationalaisafetyreport.org/publication/international-ai-safety-report-2026)

This distinction is not a diplomatic compromise between camps. It is the basis of honest risk communication. We can be confident that some AI harms exist, less confident in estimates of their scale, and much less confident about the probability of particular distant catastrophic scenarios. Different confidence levels should produce different headlines.

## Optimism has to be earned too

It would be strange to insist on rigorous evidence for every harm while accepting every promised benefit at face value. An AI model can rank candidate drugs without producing a safe medicine. It can improve a laboratory benchmark without discovering a therapy. It can generate tutoring material without demonstrating better learning outcomes for students.

The opposite mistake is to dismiss a technology because it has not yet completed the entire journey from research to deployment. Intermediate advances matter. Better tools for research, translation, accessibility, software development and administrative work can have real value before they become transformative for the whole economy.

An especially important issue is **additionality**. If an AI tool helps a team perform an activity, did it make possible something that would not otherwise have happened, or merely replace a cheap conventional technique? Did its benefits accrue to users, workers, public services or mostly the vendor? Does it perform well for people outside the high-income, English-speaking environments in which many systems are developed and evaluated?

The answer may differ between countries and communities. Some places could receive extraordinary benefits from cheaper expert assistance; others could be excluded by costs, language gaps, unreliable connectivity or institutional weakness. A global claim about the “benefits of AI” conceals those differences.

## And then there is the question of power

Even if AI produces a large increase in total wealth, we cannot infer that it will improve living standards for everyone. Who owns the models, the computing infrastructure, the distribution channels and the relevant intellectual property? Who can switch suppliers? Who has the data and capital needed to make a competitive alternative?

Some critics describe the emerging order as *technofeudalism*: an economy in which access to digital infrastructure is controlled by platform owners who collect rents rather than merely compete to sell products. The analogy draws attention to dependency and concentrated power. But it is a contested theory, not a settled diagnosis. Ordinary concepts—monopoly, oligopoly, network effects, rent-seeking, vertical integration—may explain many of the same dynamics without suggesting that capitalism has literally ended.

A different interpretation holds that AI can lower barriers to entrepreneurship: small teams gain capabilities that once required large organisations; open-weight models and competition pressure established vendors; productivity increases create new goods and businesses. There is evidence consistent with parts of that story too, though smaller firms may still face dependence on cloud providers, data sources and distribution platforms.

This is not a contest to pick the better slogan. It suggests an empirical test. Watch **market concentration, switching costs, model access, cloud dependence, profit margins, new-business formation, labour's share of income and the ability of customers to bargain**. A few powerful model providers do not automatically imply feudalism, just as a flood of AI startups does not guarantee a competitive economy.

The IMF has estimated that a large share of employment globally is exposed to AI, with substantially greater exposure in advanced economies, and warned that gains and losses could be unevenly distributed. [2](https://www.elibrary.imf.org/view/journals/006/2024/001/article-A001-en.xml) Exposure, again, is not a prediction of unemployment. But it establishes that distributional outcomes are not an eccentric side issue. They are central to the economic meaning of technological progress.

## The regulation question is harder than “more” or “less”

Consider the European Union's AI Act. By November 2026, important rules on prohibited uses, general-purpose models and transparency were already entering into application, while further high-risk obligations had later dates following 2026 amendments. [3](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/eu-ai-act-implementation-timeline) It is easy to tell a story in which this is either a victory for public protection or a drag on innovation. Both concerns deserve a hearing.

Supporters argue that accountability rules can deter unsafe deployments, clarify responsibility and protect rights that cannot be left to voluntary promises. Critics argue that broad compliance burdens can favour large firms able to hire specialist legal teams, constrain small innovators and make experimentation harder. Whether the actual rules strike a good balance is a question about their design, implementation and observed effects—not a referendum on the very idea of regulation.

There is no perfect alternative of leaving everything to the market. Companies may not bear the full costs of harms affecting people who never chose to use their products. Nor can governments simply specify every future risk in advance. Policies need independent evaluations, incident reporting, proportionate obligations, appeal routes, competition enforcement and regular review.

A particularly useful principle is that **the greater the reach, autonomy and irreversibility of a system's actions, the stronger the burden of demonstrating reliability and control should be**. A writing assistant and an autonomous system controlling critical infrastructure should not be treated as the same category of product.

## Five questions better than “Are you a doomer?”

Before accepting a claim about AI's future, I would ask:

1. **What exactly is being predicted?** A capability, a deployment, an economic effect or a social outcome?
2. **What is the evidence today?** Observed harm, a controlled study, a benchmark, a company forecast or speculation?
3. **What would prove the claim wrong?** If no outcome can change the speaker's mind, it is not a useful forecast.
4. **Who benefits or bears the risk?** Aggregate gains and local losses can coexist.
5. **Which intervention changes the odds?** Testing, market competition, auditing, liability, education or social protection—and with what unintended effects?

It is tempting to close with a verdict that makes the whole dispute manageable: AI will save us, AI will destroy us, or the answer lies politely in the middle. None is justified by current evidence. False balance would be especially dangerous if it meant treating a debunked claim and a reproducible finding as equally credible.

The more useful position is demanding rather than moderate: take documented harms seriously, investigate catastrophic possibilities without claiming certainty, test promised benefits with the same scepticism, and refuse to let arguments about the distant future excuse avoidable harm in the present.

**Hope is not an evidence category. Neither is dread. Both can motivate good questions; neither can answer them.**
