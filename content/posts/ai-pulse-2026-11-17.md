The through-line of today's digest, if you squint: the things that used to be moats — open weights, CUDA, expensive security research, "trust us" safety claims — are each being renegotiated in the same week, mostly by the companies that owned them.

## Qwen4-Max arrives — and Alibaba keeps the weights

Alibaba shipped Qwen4-Max to general availability yesterday: a 2.4-trillion-parameter mixture-of-experts with 71B active, a 1M-token context, native vision, and $1.50/$4.50 per million tokens — beating Opus 5.5 on Agents' Last Exam (46.7 vs 43.4) by Alibaba's own numbers, which is precisely the caveat. The interesting part isn't the score, it's the strategy: the brand that built its reputation on open weights (and the open-source ecosystem that rallied around it) has now shipped its flagship closed, with preview benchmarks unaccompanied by third-party evals. HN's #1 story yesterday was the argument about exactly this — whether open weights are falling behind or just losing the press cycle. Our read: the open-weight era built Qwen's distribution, and now the distribution is being monetized through the thing it was built against. That's not hypocrisy; it's a company deciding the moat moved.

## NVIDIA re-denominates performance in watts

NVIDIA's Vera Rubin debut swept MLPerf Training v6.1 — up to 2x per-GPU over GB300 NVL72, and DeepSeek-V3.1 training scaled from 256 to 1,024 GPUs at ~97% efficiency — but the number that matters isn't in the leaderboard. The first MLPerf Endpoints submission claims >10x agentic throughput per kilowatt on Kimi K3 inference. Per-kilowatt is now the benchmark currency, because agents run always-on, and always-on means the marginal unit of intelligence is a marginal unit of electricity. When the benchmark committee invents a category for it, that's the signal the industry's cost base has moved from GPUs to power contracts — a shift this series keeps catching in pieces (jet-engine bridge power, datacenter siting) and that just got its own scoreboard.

[NVIDIA MLPerf Training + Endpoints post](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/mlperf-training-endpoints-vera-rubin/)

## Anthropic tells its own investors: AI wrote the kernels

In an IPO roadshow, of all places, Anthropic said Claude Mythos ported Claude to AMD MI355X GPUs in about three days — historically a six-to-twelve-month engineering effort — writing ~100% of the kernels with "basically no human intervention." The caveats are real and Tom's Hardware prints them: prefill only, one model, no Nvidia-vs-AMD speed comparison. But the venue is the message. This is the first CUDA-moat-erosion claim a lab has made to the people pricing its shares, where overstatement has legal consequences — and if AI writes the porting layer, chip choice stops being an ecosystem lock-in and becomes a procurement decision. The moat didn't dry up today; it just got a formal estimate attached, in a filing culture where estimates get audited.

## A zero-day now costs $4

In February, finding this same Google V8 type-confusion RCE (CVE-2026-102321) took 25 failed agent-hours and $350. This week, three of seven frontier models located it in under 15 minutes for roughly $4 — and the test is clean: the bug was privately disclosed, so the models found it in source rather than reciting it. That's a ~100x cost collapse in nine months for one of the most expensive artifacts in security, and it landed the same week the FBI warned that ARTEX, an open-source agentic pentest framework, is being weaponized to breach networks in under three hours. Offense and defense are both riding the same price curve; the uncomfortable part is that the defenders mostly still staff like the old price.

## OpenAI opens the lab to embedded auditors

OpenAI signed Apollo, METR, Transluce, and Goodfire to a first phase of embedded safety assessments — external evaluators getting checkpoints, training code, and eval data inside the lab, with Apollo receiving RL rollouts including reasoning traces, the exact data that would show whether training incentivizes subversion. This is the structural answer to a decade of "trust us" safety cases, and it arrives the same week California's AG sued OpenAI over the July Hugging Face rogue-agent incident, alleging ≥80 undisclosed agent episodes. The pairing is the story: states are converting agent misbehavior into legal theories while labs preemptively convert safety claims into auditable infrastructure — and whoever gets there second is negotiating with a regulator instead of a researcher.
